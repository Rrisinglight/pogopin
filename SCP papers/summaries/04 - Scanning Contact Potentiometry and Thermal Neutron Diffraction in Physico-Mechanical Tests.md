## Abstract

The results of physical and mechanical tensile-strength tests on austenitic steel 12Kh18N10T (Cyrillic 12Х18Н10Т), in the stress range from 100 MPa to the maximum value of 700 MPa at which the sample was destroyed, are presented. Structural changes were registered synchronously by two methods: the method of scanning contact potentiometry (SCP) and the method of diffraction of thermal neutrons. At loads above 650 MPa the α-martensite phase was found in the austenite matrix, and the appearance of diffraction peaks characteristic of a cubic martensitic BCC lattice was observed in the neutron spectra. On the potentiograms this process corresponds to the appearance of local regions in which high values of electric potential gradients were observed. This is the case of discovery of the fatigue nucleus cracks in the tensile testing of steel ЭИ847 (EI847) by the method of scanning contact potentiometry.

## Keywords

method of scanning contact potentiometry; the method of thermal neutron diffraction; martensitic transformation in austenitic steel

## 1. Introduction

Diagnostics of materials during physical and mechanical testing is an established field addressing complex tasks: detection and identification of structural defects at different stages of testing, determination of the strength properties of the material, and prediction of its behaviour. Signals of electro-physical diagnostics contain a significant amount of useful information about physical processes, changes in the structure of the material, and the kinetics of destruction. The process of deformation accumulation develops in stages; each stage is controlled by certain mechanisms and gives its contribution to the overall process. Work on methods of processing electro-physical diagnostic signals aims to achieve reliable results on identification of deformation stages and damage accumulation of materials leading to destruction, for a clearer understanding of the processes and of the possibilities of external influence affecting their development.

The use of the method of scanning contact potentiometry (SCP) opens up opportunities to study, in real time, the distribution of surface stresses, deformations, mechanisms of plastic deformation, stages of development of internal defects until destruction, and other physical processes. The informative electric signal is formed at the contact of the transducer with the test object under load; **the mechanical contact surface is itself the sensitive element of the electro-physical transducer**. The number of contact spots of the converter is determined by the intensity of contact interaction in the contact zone and by the processes occurring in this area, and is substantially independent of changes in waviness and roughness parameters.

Stated advantages of SCP sensors over measuring devices of other NDT methods:

- low weight and small dimensions of the sensitive elements;
- high reliability and reproducibility of measurements owing to the small linear dimensions of the spots formed of micro-constrictions;
- high noise immunity and low noise level;
- no high-current circuits and no heated areas in the sensor design, therefore high fire safety of the measuring systems;
- low power level of the useful signal and absence of sparking in mechanical contacts;
- possibility of multi-point and distributed measurements, including time-frequency multiplexing of sensitive elements located on different portions of the test object; temporal and spectral analysis is used to code the incoming information flow.

**Purpose of this work:** joint application of SCP and thermal-neutron diffraction (Fourier stress diffractometer FSD) methods in the testing of austenitic steel 12Kh18N10T to destruction, at the pulsed fast reactor IBR-2 in FLNP JINR (Dubna, Russia).

## 2. Materials and Methods of Research

### 2.1. Method of scanning contact potentiometry

Potentiometric measurements were performed using a desktop device called **Spectroelph-FRR**, intended for spectral analysis of electrical signals [1]. The device works in conjunction with a hardware–software mobile diagnostic system hosted on an **Asus X554L** laptop. For measurements in the automated-detection regime, a **programmable amplitude discriminator (PAD)** of the measuring signals was developed, with an adjustable amplitude-discrimination scale spanning a range of **40 dB**. The discriminator cuts off the signal amplitude exceeding a predetermined level, allowing the distribution of surface electric potential to be explored for different **structural levels of signal (SLS)**. A narrowband filter allows the PAD to be configured in amplitude starting at the level of tenths of microvolts, in **0.1 µV increments**; in broadband mode the range is from units to tens of millivolts. The measuring system is controlled by the Windows OS, and the **sampling frequency of measurements is 1 Hz**.

For potentiometric measurements, sensors with electrical transducers made of steel **У10 (U10)** and **12Kh18N10T** were used. The distribution of surface potentials was obtained using the potential ground of the testing machine.

**Samples.** Cylindrical samples of steel 12Kh18N10T with a length of **85 mm** and a diameter of **5 mm** (Figure 1) were milled from rolled stock, with subsequent polishing of the working surface to a roughness **Rₐ in the range 0.55–0.70 µm**.

**Loading and scanning protocol.** The mechanical movement of the transducer over the sample surface was carried out automatically using the Spectroelph-FRR device; the stretching of the sample was performed on an **LM-29** testing machine (Table 1) [2], [3]. **On each load step the sample was held for two hours**, with **up to twenty measuring cycles** at a **linear scanning speed of 2.2 mm/s**, while the load was applied.

### 2.2. Method of Thermal Neutrons Diffraction

The diffraction of neutrons is in many respects similar to the scattering of X-rays. The velocity of thermal neutrons is sufficiently small and the energy spectrum has a continuous (Maxwellian) character, which allows analysis of the neutron energy by time-of-flight (TOF method) when the experiment is performed on a pulsed source. Depending on the neutron wavelength, the position of the diffraction peaks on the time scale is defined as (reproduced exactly as printed in the source):

$$t = (L/v) = (\lambda m L/h) = \left(2 m L d_{hkl}\right)\sin(\theta/h), \tag{1}$$

where:
- $L$ — total flight distance from neutron source to detector (m);
- $v$ — neutron velocity (m/s);
- $\lambda$ — neutron wavelength (Å);
- $m$ — neutron mass (kg);
- $h$ — Planck's constant (J·s);
- $d_{hkl}$ — the interplanar distance for crystal planes with Miller indices $(hkl)$ (Å);
- $\theta$ — Bragg angle (degrees).

The internal stresses existing in the material cause corresponding deformation of the crystal lattice, expressed as a shift of the Bragg peaks in the diffraction spectrum. When using a TOF diffractometer on a pulsed neutron source, the crystal lattice strain is determined by the relative change in the neutron time of flight Δt/t:

$$\varepsilon_{hkl} = (d_{hkl} - d^{0}_{hkl})/d^{0}_{hkl} = \Delta t / t, \tag{2}$$

where:
- $d_{hkl}$, $d^{0}_{hkl}$ — interplanar distances for the deformed and non-deformed lattices respectively (Å);
- $t$ — neutron time of flight (s);
- $\varepsilon_{hkl}$ — crystal lattice strain for the $(hkl)$ reflection (dimensionless).

From the measured residual strains, the components of the residual stress tensor can be determined:

$$\sigma_{ii} = \frac{E}{1+\nu}\left(\varepsilon_{ii} + \frac{\nu}{1-2\nu}\left(\varepsilon_X + \varepsilon_Y + \varepsilon_Z\right)\right), \tag{3}$$

where:
- $ii = X, Y, Z$ — the Cartesian index of the component;
- $\sigma_{ii}$, $\varepsilon_{ii}$ — components of the stress (MPa) and strain (dimensionless) tensors, respectively;
- $E$ — the Young's modulus (GPa);
- $\nu$ — Poisson's ratio (dimensionless).

The essence of the diffraction method for studying stresses in the standard experimental layout consists in the formation of incident and scattered neutron beams by means of diaphragms or radial collimators, and in the selection of a small scattering volume in the sample (Figure 2). The relative displacements of the diffraction peaks from the positions determined by the unit-cell parameters of the undeformed material are measured, which, according to formula (2), is recalculated into strain in a direction parallel to the neutron scattering vector **Q**.

In addition, analysis of the shape (in the simplest case the width) of the diffraction peaks makes it possible to estimate the magnitude of crystal lattice distortions within individual grains (microstrains) and the sizes of coherent scattering domains. This is particularly convenient on a TOF diffractometer, based on the functional dependence of peak width on interplanar distance [4] (reproduced exactly as printed in the source):

$$W^2 = C_1 + C_2 d^2 + C_3 d^2 + C_4 d^4, \tag{4}$$

where:
- $W$ — peak width (in units of $d$, Å);
- $C_1$ and $C_2$ — constants determining the resolution function of the diffractometer, known from measurements with a standard sample;
- $C_3 = (\Delta a/a)^2$ — dispersion of the unit cell parameter (microstrain), dimensionless;
- $C_4$ — constant related to the size of the crystallites;
- $d$ — interplanar spacing (Å).

**The FSD diffractometer.** In the Frank Laboratory of Neutron Physics of the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (Dubna, Russia), at the long-pulse neutron source — the **IBR-2 reactor** — a specialized neutron **Fourier stress diffractometer FSD** was designed to investigate internal stresses in bulk industrial samples and thermo-mechanical properties of structural materials.

A unique method of neutron correlation time-of-flight diffractometry [5] — the use of a **fast Fourier chopper** for primary neutron beam intensity modulation and the **RTOF method** for data acquisition — makes it possible to obtain diffraction spectra on FSD with the required high resolution (**Δd/d ≈ 2×10⁻³ at a scattering angle 2θ = 140°** and **Δd/d ≈ 4×10⁻³ at scattering angles 2θ = ±90°**) over a wide range of interplanar distances, providing the necessary accuracy in registering small displacements of diffraction peaks and their broadening. The FSD experimental equipment includes a precise **Huber goniometer with a carrying capacity up to 300 kg**, a **high-temperature mirror furnace allowing samples to be heated up to 1000 °C**, and automated diaphragms.

A distinctive feature of FSD is the use of **two detectors at scattering angles ±90° with large solid angles (~0.1 sr each)** and high resolution. A system of **radial collimators with a wide aperture** installed in front of the 90°-detectors provides a **minimum scattering volume of 2×2×2 mm** in the sample, in which the crystal lattice strain is measured (Figure 2). This substantially increases the luminosity of the experiment through full use of the detector system and enables effective study of residual stresses in **thick samples (up to 30 mm)**.

One advantage of neutron TOF diffraction is the wide interval of interplanar distances, allowing a set of diffraction peaks to be registered simultaneously. This makes it possible to study the behaviour of polycrystalline materials with rather complex structure, including multiphase ones, in fixed geometry under various external conditions [6].

To study the behaviour of structural materials under external load and high temperature directly in the neutron beam on FSD **in situ**, the mechanical-type **LM-29** testing machine is used. It allows the sample to be subjected to **uniaxial tension or compression up to 29 kN** and **heating up to 800 °C by passing electric current through the sample**. The control system allows forming any necessary combination of external load and temperature values on the sample. As a rule, in such experiments the detectors at scattering angles 2θ = ±90° detect the diffraction spectra at certain values of the load on the sample. At the same time, **two independent components of deformation (longitudinal and transverse) are measured simultaneously**, parallel and perpendicular to the neutron scattering vector **Q**.

## 3. Experimental Results and Discussion

### 3.1. Results of Scanning Contact Potentiometry method

Registration of the electric potential difference on the sample surface and analysis of diagnostic information were produced on the hardware–software complex **"ElphysLAB-IDS"** mobile information and diagnostic system (IDS). A software package including an IDS calculation software module allows the user to remotely provide processing of incoming information, to form a database, and to make the necessary calculations for visualization of the obtained results and diagnosis.

When the load was increased, a colour gamut of changes in the surface potential was observed on the potentiogram (Figure 3), caused by the influence of mechanical stresses and by the formation of dynamic roughness. This was particularly noticeable under loads exceeding the yield strength. The analysis of the measurement results used the PAD with its broad functionality.

In the upper panel of Figure 3, at 400 MPa, the process of concentration of internal stresses at three locations on the sample is presented (red–yellow tones). At the left and right sides of the specimen the stress concentration occurs near the throat, in the region of transition from the diameter of the working part of the specimen to the diameter of the threaded part; in the centre — where the most effective stress occurs. In the process of plastic flow, zones of localization of macro-deformation are established, clearly visible at a load of 600 and especially at 650 MPa. In these zones, at this and higher loads, formation of a local constriction (neck) occurs.

High-resolution measurement (**SLS ≈ 10 microvolts**) enables definition of a local region with relatively high values of internal stresses (**stresses of the III kind**) in the region of elastic and quasi-elastic deformation (Figure 4 b and Figure 5 b).

At a stress of **200 MPa** the trend of concentration of internal stresses at three locations on the sample is visible (Figure 5.b), the same as in Figure 3. At higher loads the colour pattern undergoes significant changes at the level of microplastic deformation (Figure 6.b): high stresses start to concentrate mainly on the right part of the specimen, spreading to the middle and beyond. A small linear region with a high value of the potential at the left side of the sample (Figure 6.a and Figure 7.a) consequently did not have a noticeable impact on the formation of the neck and disappeared at higher loads.

**Joint analysis of the two methods** suggests that the formation of the α'-martensite phase apparently already began at loads of **400–600 MPa**, when the potentiograms began to show alternating local areas in the form of multi-coloured spots (Figure 7.b and Figure 8.c) in which the gradients of the electric potential are associated with a high rate of change of potential polarity. This does not contradict the theory of martensite deformation [7], according to which the role of plastic deformation in shear transformation is varied:

1. It may be associated with a gradual increase in the level of internal stresses before the appearance of martensite stress and overcoming the resistance of an accommodation.
2. As the load increases, martensite nuclei arise, corresponding to a particular type of defect. In austenitic steels of this class, nuclei are **α'-martensite plates with a thickness of 5–7 nm**, which appear at the junction of two avalanche dislocations or when two stacks of stacking faults intersect in the slip band.
3. The medium in which the crystal grows changes, which significantly affects the kinetics of the process. The latter apparently has a special influence on the morphology of the **electrical double layer**, in which the mentioned high-speed inversion of the potential sign occurs.

The beginning and development of the localization process of microdeformation can be observed in the measurements with relatively low SLS values (Figure 8.a, Figure 9.a and Figure 10.a), while **the appearance of nuclei of martensite is visible only when SLS ≤ 20 µV**. Within the framework of the mathematical model of the electrical micro-contact [8] and models of the deformation activity of metal surfaces [9], this is due to the relatively low amplitude and energy of the mechanical waves generated by the sources of internal stresses formed around the nuclei of martensite.

Figure 11 shows the potential on the surface of **sample № 2 (with a welded seam in the middle)**, for SLS 10 µV and five measurement cycles. For loads larger than 100 MPa there is a clear border separating the two halves of the sample. The welded seam damps external tensile stresses; for this reason, the potential value is lower on the left side of the sample than on the right. The boundary gradually begins to erode under the influence of the resulting martensite, whose appearance can also be judged from a sharp change in the character of the curves of the deterministic signal component at **600–700 MPa** loads (Figure 12).

The regions with a high potential gradient shown in Figure 10.c can also be associated with the resulting micro-nucleus observed during the fatigue tests of **ЭИ847 (EI847)** steel [see 9]. The structure of the diagnostic signal was analysed at different SLS, starting from values of the order of **0.5 µV**. It has been established that the **first- and second-order stresses (by Davidenkov's criterion)** most significantly affect the sign and amplitude of the diagnostic signal, while the low-amplitude component of the signal, associated with strains of the third kind, is practically always screened. The potentiograms shown in Figure 13 were constructed by mathematical extrapolation of the results of point-wise scanning **along eight measuring tracks** to the entire working surface of the sample.

### 3.2. The results of neutron diffraction

Neutron diffraction experiments were performed on the Fourier stress diffractometer FSD at the IBR-2 pulsed reactor in FLNP JINR (Dubna). During the experiment the investigated 12Kh18N10T steel samples were subjected to external uniaxial tensile load **in situ** in the neutron beam using the LM-29 testing machine. Simultaneously with the diffraction spectra, the data of the scanning contact potentiometry were independently recorded.

- **Sample № 1** was subjected to a load in the range **from 100 to 700 MPa**, before the formation of a noticeable (visible) neck in the sample. The last measurement was carried out at a load **reduced to 650 MPa** in order to avoid the destruction of the sample.
- **Sample № 2**, with a welded seam, was subjected to a load in the range **from 100 to 700 MPa until the sample was completely destroyed**. The last measurement was carried out with the sample completely destroyed (rupture) with the load removed (**σ = 0 MPa**).

Diffraction spectra were recorded by detectors at scattering angles **2θ = ±90°** for each load value on the sample (Figure 14). The measured spectra were processed by profile analysis according to the **Rietveld method** [10], yielding the values of the crystal lattice parameters of the material and the parameters of broadening of the diffraction peaks. The **longitudinal strain (∥) was registered by the AL detector (2Θ = +90°)**, and the **perpendicular (⊥) by the AR detector (2Θ = −90°)**. The crystal lattice strain for the austenite phase was calculated as ε = (a − a₀)/a₀, where a and a₀ are the parameters of the crystal lattice of the deformed and undeformed materials, respectively.

The diffraction peaks from the initial austenitic steel corresponded to the **cubic FCC lattice (sp. gr. Fm3̄m)**. With increasing degree of plastic deformation of the material at **loads above 650 MPa**, the formation of the **α'-martensite phase** was detected in the austenite matrix, while the appearance of diffraction peaks characteristic of a **cubic martensitic BCC lattice (sp. gr. Im3̄m)** was observed in the neutron spectra (Figure 15). Analysis of the intensities of the diffraction peaks in the spectra showed the presence of a **noticeable texture in the initial sample of austenitic steel**, and a **strong texture is also observed in the martensite formed**.

Estimates for the elasticity modulus and Poisson's ratio for the austenitic phase were made from the linear approximation of the ε(σ) dependences in the elastic strain region (**up to 500 MPa**): **E = 182 GPa and ν = 0.29 for sample № 1**; **E = 195 GPa and ν = 0.30 for sample № 2** (Figure 16). At loads greater than 500 MPa the deformation of the material was plastic, and there was a noticeable broadening of the diffraction peaks, increasing with increasing external load, due to an increase in the dislocation density in the material. From the broadening of the diffraction peaks, the value of the crystal lattice microstrain was estimated as a function of the applied load (Figure 17). At **loads in excess of 700 MPa** the formation of a **neck on the right side of the specimen** was visually observed, indicating the localization of plastic deformation in this region and the termination of plastic deformation in the remaining volume of the sample.

### Comparison of the two methods

The two techniques are applied synchronously to the same loading sequence so that the surface electro-physical signature (SCP) can be calibrated against the bulk crystallographic state (neutron diffraction). The correlation reported by the authors is as follows:

- **Diffraction** establishes the **threshold for detectable α'-martensite (BCC peaks) at loads above 650 MPa**, and gives the quantitative bulk parameters: lattice strain ε(σ), E, ν, and the averaged microstrain, which jumps sharply above 500–600 MPa.
- **SCP**, at high resolution (SLS ≤ 20 µV), shows the **precursor stage of the same transformation already at 400–600 MPa**, as alternating local multi-coloured regions in which electric-potential gradients arise from a high rate of change of potential polarity — interpreted as the nuclei of α'-martensite, whose 5–7 nm plates generate mechanical waves too weak in amplitude and energy to appear at coarser SLS.
- The microstrain rise measured from diffraction peak broadening (Figure 17) and the sharp change of the deterministic SCP signal component (Figure 12) occur over the same load interval (600–700 MPa).
- The localization of plastic deformation seen in the SCP maps (zones at 600 and especially 650 MPa, right-hand part of the specimen) coincides with the region where necking and subsequent destruction occurred, and with the diffraction observation that plastic deformation localizes on the right side of the specimen above 700 MPa.

## Key numerical values and experimental conditions

**Material and samples**

- Steel grade: austenitic **12Kh18N10T** (Cyrillic 12Х18Н10Т); the sensor transducers were made of steels **У10 (U10)** and 12Kh18N10T.
- Sample geometry (Figure 1): cylindrical, **overall length 85 mm (85 max)**, **working-part diameter 5 mm (Ø5±0.1)**, **minimum working length 50 mm**, thread **M12** with thread length **17.5 mm**, fillet **R3 at two places**.
- Manufacturing: milled from rolled stock, working surface polished to **Rₐ = 0.55–0.70 µm**.
- Sample № 1: solid, tested 100 → 700 MPa, final measurement at the load reduced to **650 MPa** (kept intact).
- Sample № 2: **welded from two halves using a fibre-laser welding process**, with the seam in the middle; tested 100 → 700 MPa to complete destruction; final measurement on the ruptured sample at **σ = 0 MPa**.

**Loading**

- Stress range examined: **100 MPa to 700 MPa** (SCP potentiograms additionally presented at **750 MPa**).
- **Hold time at each load step: 2 hours.**
- **Up to 20 measuring cycles** per load step (Figure 10 uses 10 cycles; Figure 11 uses 5 cycles).
- Testing machine **LM-29**: ±29 kN, up to 800 °C (by passing electric current through the sample), sample length range 30–100 mm, mechanical extensometer, PC control (Windows/VME).

**SCP instrumentation**

- Device: **Spectroelph-FRR** desktop spectral-analysis instrument; hardware–software complex **"ElphysLAB-IDS"** mobile information and diagnostic system, hosted on an **Asus X554L** laptop, Windows OS.
- **Programmable amplitude discriminator (PAD)**: adjustable amplitude-discrimination scale over a **40 dB** range; narrowband configuration from tenths of a microvolt in **0.1 µV** steps; broadband from units to tens of millivolts.
- **Sampling frequency: 1 Hz.**
- **Linear scanning speed of the transducer: 2.2 mm/s**; scan duration per track 24 s (horizontal axis of all potentiograms).
- SLS levels used in the figures: **10⁻¹ V; 2·10⁻⁵ V (20 µV); 1.5·10⁻⁵ V (15 µV); 1.0·10⁻⁵ V (10 µV)**; for the EI847 data of Figure 13 also **3.7·10⁻⁴ V** and **5·10⁻⁶ V (5 µV)**.
- Resolution needed to see martensite nuclei: **SLS ≤ 20 µV**; resolution quoted for identifying local regions of high internal stress: **SLS ≈ 10 µV** (conclusions: SLS ≤ 10 µV).
- Signal structure analysed down to **≈ 0.5 µV**.
- Figure 13 potentiograms extrapolated from point-wise scanning along **eight measuring tracks**.

**Neutron diffraction instrumentation (FSD, IBR-2, FLNP JINR Dubna)**

- Resolution: **Δd/d ≈ 2×10⁻³ at 2θ = 140°**; **Δd/d ≈ 4×10⁻³ at 2θ = ±90°**.
- **Two detectors at 2θ = ±90°**, solid angle **≈ 0.1 sr each**; AL detector at **2Θ = +90°** gives the longitudinal (∥) strain, AR detector at **2Θ = −90°** the transverse (⊥) strain.
- Radial collimators define a minimum gauge (scattering) volume of **2×2×2 mm**.
- Suitable for residual-stress work in samples up to **30 mm** thick.
- **Huber goniometer, carrying capacity up to 300 kg**; high-temperature mirror furnace up to **1000 °C**; automated diaphragms.
- **Fast Fourier chopper** for primary-beam intensity modulation; **RTOF** data acquisition (correlation TOF diffractometry).
- Data reduction by **Rietveld profile refinement**.
- Measured d-range of the presented spectra: **≈ 0.6 to 2.2 Å**.

**Crystallography and derived quantities**

- Initial austenite: **cubic FCC, space group Fm3̄m**; reflections observed A(111) d ≈ 2.08 Å, A(200) d ≈ 1.80 Å, A(220) d ≈ 1.27 Å, A(311) d ≈ 1.085 Å, A(222) d ≈ 1.04 Å, A(400) d ≈ 0.90 Å.
- **α'-martensite: cubic BCC, space group Im3̄m**; reflections observed M(110) d ≈ 2.03–2.05 Å, M(200) d ≈ 1.44 Å, M(211) d ≈ 1.17–1.18 Å, M(220) d ≈ 0.92 Å.
- **α'-martensite detected at loads above 650 MPa** (diffraction); the precursor nucleation stage inferred from SCP at **400–600 MPa**.
- α'-martensite nuclei: plates of **5–7 nm thickness**, forming at the junction of two avalanche dislocations or at the intersection of two stacks of stacking faults in a slip band.
- Elastic constants from the linear fit of ε(σ) up to **500 MPa**: **sample № 1 — E = 182 GPa, ν = 0.29; sample № 2 — E = 195 GPa, ν = 0.30**.
- Lattice strain at maximum load: sample № 1 ε∥ up to **+5.10×10⁻³**, ε⊥ down to **−1.65×10⁻³**; sample № 2 ε∥ ≈ **+4.0×10⁻³**, ε⊥ ≈ **−1.7×10⁻³** at 700 MPa, with post-fracture residual values ε∥ = +0.40×10⁻³ and ε⊥ = +0.23×10⁻³ at σ = 0.
- Averaged (hkl) microstrain: plateau at **≈ 1.4–1.55×10⁻³** from 100 to 500 MPa for both samples; rising to **≈ 3.65×10⁻³** (sample № 1, final 650 MPa measurement) and **≈ 2.85–2.97×10⁻³** (sample № 2 at 700 MPa and after destruction).
- Neck formation observed visually at loads in excess of **700 MPa**, on the **right side** of the specimen.
- Reference steel for the fatigue-crack-nucleus comparison: **ЭИ847 (EI847)**, with stress–strain curve reaching ≈ 680 MPa at ≈ 0.095 strain and fracturing at ≈ 0.13 strain; potentiogram fragments at 525, 550, 590, 650, 680 and 630 MPa.

## Conclusions (as stated by the authors)

1. For the first time, in comprehensive studies of austenitic steel 12Kh18N10T, the methods of scanning contact potentiometry and diffraction of thermal neutrons on the Fourier stress diffractometer FSD were tested on an LM-29 testing machine at the pulsed fast reactor IBR-2 in FLNP JINR, and unique results were obtained.

2. The formation of an α'-martensite phase was detected by the method of thermal neutron diffraction at loads above 650 MPa in the austenite matrix. The presented analysis of the results by the two methods makes it possible to estimate the initial stage of the formation of α'-martensite nuclei, which is already observed at 400–600 MPa on potentiograms in the form of alternating local regions in which the electric potential gradients are caused by a high rate of change of its polarity.

3. Linear approximation of the dependences ε(σ) in the deformation area corresponding to stresses up to 500 MPa gives values for the modulus of elasticity and Poisson's ratio of the austenitic phase: E = 182 GPa and ν = 0.29 for sample № 1, and E = 195 GPa and ν = 0.30 for sample № 2.

4. Electro-physical measurements conducted on the hardware–software complex "ElphysLAB-IDS" using the desktop device Spectroelph-FRR, with a resolution SLS ≤ 10 µV, help to identify local areas with relatively high values of internal stresses in the region of elastic and quasi-elastic deformation. In the process of plastic flow, zones of localization of macrodeformation are formed in the sample volume, clearly manifested in the potentiographs at a stress of 600 and especially at 650 MPa. In one of these areas the neck later formed and destruction of the sample occurred.

5. The beginning and development of the localization process of microdeformation is observed in the measurements with relatively low values of SLS, whereas the appearance of martensite nuclei is registered at SLS ≤ 20 µV, due to the relatively low amplitude and energy of the mechanical waves generated by the sources of internal stresses formed around the nuclei of martensite.

6. The results of the tensile testing of the sample made of 12Kh18N10T steel, welded from two halves using a fibre-laser welding process, show that in the range of loads from 200 to 700 MPa the weld seam affects the internal stress at the half of the sample which is located behind the weld relative to the active grip of the testing machine.

7. The methodology of complex research using the methods of scanning contact potentiometry and diffraction of thermal neutrons at the pulsed fast reactor IBR-2 in FLNP JINR in Dubna makes it possible to address, at a high technical level, both fundamental materials-science challenges and the development of tools and techniques of electro-physical diagnostics and non-destructive testing.
