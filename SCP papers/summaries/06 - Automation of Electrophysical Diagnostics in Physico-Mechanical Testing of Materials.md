## Abstract

The paper reports the results of applying a **benchtop instrument for spectral analysis of functional electrophysical diagnostic signals, Spectroelph-FRR**. The instrument is intended to work together with **tensile testing machines that clamp specimens horizontally**. During materials testing, the **scanning transducer slides translationally in the horizontal direction along the specimen surface**. The informative electrical signal is generated **at the contact spots between the transducer and the object under test while the object is under load**; the surface of the mechanical contact itself constitutes the **sensing element of the electrophysical transducer**. The number of contact spots of the transducer is determined by the intensity of the contact interaction in the contact zone and by the processes taking place in that zone, and also depends substantially on changes in the **waviness and roughness** parameters. To investigate the **internal (residual) stresses** arising in the bulk of a specimen of steel **12Kh18N10T** (12Х18Н10Т), the **thermal-neutron diffraction method** was also used at the Frank Laboratory of Neutron Physics of the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (Dubna, Russia).

## Keywords

Electrophysical diagnostics; physico-mechanical testing of materials; thermal-neutron diffraction method; scanning contact potentiometry method.

## 1. The scanning contact potentiometry method

Diagnostics of materials during physico-mechanical testing involves solving a number of difficult problems of **detecting and identifying structural defects at different stages of testing**, with the aim of determining the strength properties of the material and predicting its behaviour. Signals of electrophysical diagnostics carry a substantial amount of useful information about physical processes, changes in the structure of the material, the kinetics of fracture, and so on. The accumulation of deformation develops **in stages**, each stage being governed by particular mechanisms and contributing its own share to the overall process. Work on creating means and methods for processing electrophysical diagnostic signals pursues the goal of achieving reliable identification of the stages of accumulation of deformation damage leading to fracture, in order to understand more clearly the processes taking place and the possibility of influencing their development from outside.

The use of the **scanning contact potentiometry (SCP)** method opens up broad possibilities for studying, **in real time**, the surface distribution of stresses and strains, the mechanisms of plastic deformation, the stages of development of internal defects up to the fracture of the material, and other physical processes.

**Measurement principle.** The informative electrical signal is generated **at the contact spots between the transducer and the object under test while the latter is under load**; the surface of the mechanical contact thereby acts as the **sensing element of the electrophysical transducer**. The number of contact spots of the transducer is determined by the **intensity of the contact interaction in the contact zone** and by the processes occurring in that zone, and it also depends substantially on changes in the **waviness and roughness** parameters of the surface.

**Advantages of contact-potentiometry sensors** over the measuring devices of other non-destructive testing (NDT) methods, as listed by the authors:

- **Small mass and overall dimensions of the sensing elements**, a consequence of the small linear dimensions of the microcontacts that form;
- **High reliability and reproducibility** of the measurement results;
- **High immunity to interference and a low level of intrinsic noise**;
- **High fire safety** — the sensor designs contain no high-current circuits and no heated regions, so the measuring systems are fire-safe;
- **Low power level of the useful signal** and, consequently, **no sparking in the mechanical contacts**;
- The SCP method supports **multipoint and distributed measurements**, including the use of **frequency-time multiplexing of sensing elements** located at different sites on the object under test.

For processing the incoming data stream, **program codes for time-domain and spectral analysis** are used.

### 1.1 The Spectroelph-FRR instrument and its measuring system

Potentiometric measurements were performed with the **benchtop instrument Spectroelph-FRR**, designed for the spectral analysis of electrical signals [1]. The instrument works jointly with the **hardware–software complex of a mobile diagnostic system** hosted on an **Asus X554L laptop**.

For measurements in **automated detection mode**, a **programmable amplitude discriminator** of the measurement signals was developed, with an **adjustable amplitude-discrimination scale spanning 40 dB**. The discriminator cuts off signal amplitude values exceeding a preset level, which makes it possible to study the distribution of the surface electric potential **at different structural levels of the signal**. The **narrowband filter** allows the discriminator to be set by amplitude **starting from the level of tenths of a microvolt, with a step of 0.1 µV**; the **broadband** channel allows setting **from units up to tens of millivolts**. The measuring system runs under **Windows**; the **measurement sampling rate is 1 Hz**.

For the potentiometric measurements, sensors with **electrophysical transducers made of U10 steel (У10)** were used. The surface potential distribution was obtained **relative to the "ground" potential of the tensile testing machine**.

**Scanning kinematics.** During the tests, the scanning transducer **slides translationally in the horizontal direction along the specimen surface**. This motion is produced by a **screw (lead-screw) mechanism with a mechanical drive from a stepper motor**. The **carriage** carrying the electrophysical sensor translates along **two cylindrical guides**. **Three rotational bearings are used on each side** of the screw mechanism and of the guides. A **telescopic measuring rod** with the **needle-type transducer** is mounted on a **cantilever** and is pressed against the specimen surface by a **weight fixed on a coil spring**. The general appearance of the information-and-measurement system with the Spectroelph-FRR instrument is shown in Fig. 1.

**Signal chain.** The measuring system for electrophysical diagnostics and non-destructive testing consists of a **low-voltage input-signal amplifier** and a **microcontroller with a built-in ADC**. The precision ADC is integrated in a single package together with a **programmable pre-amplifier**. For the experiment, a **32-bit ADC ADS1262 from Texas Instruments** was chosen. At a **sampling rate of 2.5 SPS**, the ADC has a resolution of **23.5 noise-free bits**, which — with a **reference voltage of 2.5 V** and a **pre-amplification gain of about 30** — yields a **limiting resolution of ~7 nV**. The programmable gain makes it possible to widen the input-voltage range considerably. **Data transfer is over the SPI protocol.**

### 1.2 Specimens and loading conditions

**Cylindrical specimens of steel 12Kh18N10T (12Х18Н10Т), 85 mm long and 5 mm in diameter**, were manufactured by **milling from rolled stock** with subsequent **polishing of the working surface to a roughness Ra varying within 0.55–0.70 µm**.

The mechanical displacement of the sensor over the specimen surface was performed **automatically by the Spectroelph-FRR instrument** while the specimen was being **stretched on an LM-29 loading machine** [2, 3]. **At each load level the specimen was held for 2 hours**, during which **up to 20 measurement cycles** were carried out at a **linear scanning speed of 2.2 mm/s**.

## 2. Results of the experiment by the scanning contact potentiometry method

Recording of the difference of electric potentials on the specimen surface and the analysis of the diagnostic information were performed with the **ElphysLAB-IDS (ElphysLAB-ИДС) hardware–software complex**. The software component of the complex makes it possible to **process the incoming information remotely, build a database, and carry out the calculations required to visualize the results obtained and to formulate diagnoses**.

In the course of the experiment, the **increase of load was reflected in the potentiogram as changes in the colour pattern of the surface potential** (Fig. 2), caused by the influence of the mechanical stresses and by the **formation of dynamic roughness**. This was **particularly noticeable at loads exceeding the yield strength**. For the analysis of the measurement results, the **programmable amplitude discriminator with wide functional capabilities** was used.

In the **upper fragment of Fig. 2, at 400 MPa**, the process of **concentration of internal stresses in three regions of the specimen (regions 1, 2, 3)** is visible. On the **left and right sides** of the specimen, the stress concentration is related to the **shape of the specimen** — namely, to the **fillet at the transition from the diameter of the working part of the specimen to the diameter of the threaded part**; in the **centre**, it is related to the **largest effective stresses arising under tension**.

During plastic flow, **zones of strain localization** are formed, which is clearly visible **at a stress of 400 MPa**. At a **higher load**, the formation of a **local contraction, or neck**, begins.

## 3. The thermal-neutron diffraction method

At the **Frank Laboratory of Neutron Physics of the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (Dubna)**, at the **long-pulse neutron source — the IBR-2 pulsed reactor** — a **specialized neutron Fourier stress diffractometer (FSD)** has been built and is in operation. It is intended for investigating **internal (residual) stresses in bulk industrial specimens and components** and for studying the **thermo-mechanical properties of structural materials**.

The **unique technique of neutron correlation time-of-flight diffractometry** [3, 4] — the use of a **fast Fourier chopper to modulate the intensity of the primary neutron beam** together with the **RTOF method of data accumulation** — makes it possible to obtain, on the Fourier stress diffractometer, diffraction spectra with the required resolution:

$$\frac{\Delta d}{d} \approx 2 \times 10^{-3} \quad \text{at} \quad 2\theta = 140^{\circ}$$

$$\frac{\Delta d}{d} \approx 4 \times 10^{-3} \quad \text{at} \quad 2\theta = \pm 90^{\circ}$$

where $d$ is the interplanar spacing (Å), $\Delta d$ is the width (uncertainty) of the measured interplanar spacing (Å), $\Delta d/d$ is the relative resolution of the diffractometer (dimensionless), and $2\theta$ is the scattering angle (degrees). This resolution is achieved **over a wide range of interplanar spacings**, which provides the accuracy needed to register **small shifts of the diffraction peaks and their broadenings**.

## 4. Results of the experiment by the thermal-neutron diffraction method

The neutron-diffraction experiments were performed on the **Fourier stress diffractometer at the IBR-2 pulsed reactor at FLNP JINR (Dubna)**. During the experiment, the **12Kh18N10T steel specimens under study were subjected to an external uniaxial tensile load in situ in the neutron beam** by means of the **LM-29 loading machine**. During the neutron experiment, **scanning contact potentiometry data were recorded independently and simultaneously with the diffraction spectra**.

The specimen was **loaded in the range 100–700 MPa, up to the formation of a noticeable neck**. The **last measurement was carried out at a reduced load of 650 MPa** in order to avoid fracture of the specimen.

The diffraction spectra were recorded by **detectors at scattering angles $2\theta = \pm 90^{\circ}$ at every value of the load on the specimen** (Fig. 3).

The measured spectra were processed by **profile analysis using the Rietveld method**, as a result of which the values of the **crystal-lattice parameters of the material** and the **parameters of diffraction-peak broadening** were obtained.

The diffraction peaks from the **initial austenitic steel corresponded to the cubic FCC lattice (space group $Fm\bar{3}m$)**. **With increasing degree of plastic deformation of the material, at loads above 650 MPa, the formation of the α′-martensite phase in the austenitic matrix was detected**; the neutron spectra then showed the appearance of diffraction peaks characteristic of the **cubic martensitic BCC lattice (space group $Im\bar{3}m$)**.

Analysis of the **intensities of the diffraction peaks in the spectra showed the presence of appreciable texture in the initial austenitic-steel specimen**; **strong texture is also observed in the martensite that formed**.

A single rectangular greyscale contour map (a filled-contour "colour picture" of the surface potential, reproduced in greyscale in the journal).

- **Horizontal axis:** «время, с» — time, s, from **0 to 24 s**, with major ticks every 2 s. Because the transducer slides at a constant 2.2 mm/s, this time axis maps directly onto position along the specimen surface.
- **Vertical axis:** «циклы» — measurement cycles, from **0 to 20**, labelled every 5 cycles.
- **Top annotations:** the numerals **1, 2 and 3** mark the three regions of the specimen discussed in the text — region 1 occupying roughly the first third of the scan (≈0–8 s), region 2 the middle (≈8–16 s), region 3 the final third (≈16–24 s).

The map is built from about twenty successive scans stacked one above the other. In regions 1 and 2 the field is dominated by broad, smooth grey bands running roughly vertically (i.e. persisting from cycle to cycle at a fixed position along the specimen), interrupted by **isolated near-black spots** — the strongest potential extrema — located approximately at t ≈ 1 s (cycles 1–3), t ≈ 7 s (cycles 18–19), t ≈ 9–11 s (cycles 9–12), t ≈ 11 s (cycles 2–4) and t ≈ 13 s (cycle 0). These dark islands are the sites where the internal stresses concentrate. From roughly **t ≈ 15–16 s onward (region 3)** the texture changes character sharply: the broad bands break up into a **fine-grained, strongly striped pattern of alternating light and dark cells** repeating on a scale of 1–2 s along the time axis and of a few cycles along the vertical axis — the signature of the developing dynamic roughness and strain localization at this end of the specimen.

### Figure 3 — "Measured time-of-flight diffraction spectra from specimen No. 1 for the left AL (a) and right AR (b) detectors. Miller indices are given for the main peaks of austenite and of the martensite formed as a result of plastic deformation" (p. 63)

Labelled reflections: **A(400)** at ≈0.90 Å (≈1.3), **A(222)** at ≈1.04 Å (≈2.7), **A(311)** at ≈1.08 Å (≈10), **M(211)** at ≈1.17 Å (≈4.9), **A(220)** at ≈1.27 Å (≈12.3, the tallest peak here — whereas in the AL detector A(220) is barely 1.4), **M(200)** at ≈1.43 Å (≈1.8, absent from the AL panel), **A(200)** at ≈1.79 Å (≈4.4), **M(110)** at ≈2.03 Å (≈1.5) and **A(111)** at ≈2.08 Å (≈4.9 — against ≈11.8 in AL). ## Key numerical values and experimental conditions

**Specimens**

- Material: steel **12Kh18N10T** (12Х18Н10Т), austenitic stainless steel; the Fig. 2 caption prints the grade as 12Х18Н10 (without the final Т).
- Geometry: **cylindrical, length 85 mm, diameter 5 mm**.
- Manufacture: **milled from rolled stock**, working surface subsequently **polished to Ra = 0.55–0.70 µm**.
- Specimen geometry feature relevant to the results: a **fillet at the transition from the working-part diameter to the threaded-part diameter** at each end.

**Electrophysical (SCP) measurements**

- Transducer: **needle type, ⌀2–3 mm**, material **steel U7–U10 (GOST 1435-99)**; the sensors actually used had transducers of **U10 steel**.
- **Measuring rod length: 30 mm**, telescopic, cantilever-mounted, pressed to the surface by a weight on a coil spring.
- **Sliding speed: maximum 2.2 mm/s, minimum 0.5 mm/s**; the reported tests were run at the **linear scanning speed of 2.2 mm/s**.
- **Transducer sensitivity: 0.01 µV.**
- **Hold time at each load: 2 hours**, with **up to 20 measurement cycles** per load.
- **Acquisition sampling rate: 1 Hz**; potentiogram time axis spanning **0–24 s** per scan, **0–20 cycles** per potentiogram.
- **Amplitude discrimination range: 40 dB**; narrowband setting from **tenths of a microvolt in steps of 0.1 µV**; broadband setting from **units to tens of millivolts**.
- **ADC:** Texas Instruments **ADS1262, 32-bit**; **2.5 SPS → 23.5 noise-free bits**; **Vref = 2.5 V**; **pre-amplifier gain ≈30**; **limiting resolution ≈7 nV**; **SPI** data transfer.
- Reference: potentials measured **relative to the "ground" potential of the tensile testing machine**.
- **Loading machine: LM-29.**
- Stress level illustrated in the potentiogram: **400 MPa** (above the yield strength; strain-localization zones clearly visible; necking begins at higher load).

**Neutron-diffraction measurements**

- Facility: **IBR-2 pulsed reactor**, long-pulse neutron source, **FLNP JINR, Dubna**; **Fourier stress diffractometer (FSD)**.
- Technique: **neutron correlation time-of-flight diffractometry** with a **fast Fourier chopper** modulating the primary-beam intensity and the **RTOF** data-accumulation method.
- Resolution: **Δd/d ≈ 2 × 10⁻³ at 2θ = 140°**; **Δd/d ≈ 4 × 10⁻³ at 2θ = ±90°**.
- Detector positions used in this experiment: **2θ = ±90°** (detectors AL and AR), a spectrum recorded **at every load value**.
- Loading: **uniaxial tension in situ in the neutron beam** on the **LM-29** machine, **load range 100–700 MPa**, up to the formation of a **noticeable neck**; **final measurement at a reduced load of 650 MPa** to avoid fracturing the specimen.
- Phases: initial **austenite, FCC, space group Fm3̄m**; **α′-martensite, BCC, space group Im3̄m**, detected **at loads above 650 MPa**.
- Data reduction: **Rietveld profile analysis**, yielding **crystal-lattice parameters** and **diffraction-peak broadening parameters**.
- Diffraction-spectrum ranges as plotted: **d ≈ 0.6–2.2 Å**, **intensity 0–~12 × 10⁴ a.u.**
- SCP data were recorded **independently and simultaneously** with the diffraction spectra during the neutron experiment.

## Conclusions (as stated by the authors)

1. The **scanning contact potentiometry method makes it possible, during tensile testing of 12Kh18N10T steel specimens, to detect the sites of plastic deformation**.
2. The use of the **amplitude-discrimination method when processing the results** makes it possible — **already in the elastic-deformation region and over the whole length of the specimen** — to identify the particular region at which the **neck subsequently formed** (that is, the region of plastic deformation).
3. In tensile tests of 12Kh18N10T steel specimens, **thermal-neutron diffraction revealed a change of the metal's structure (austenite/martensite) at the sites of plastic deformation**.
