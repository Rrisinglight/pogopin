## 1. Technical field

The invention relates to the field of **non-destructive testing (NDT) and diagnostics** and may be used for detecting **local flaws**, in particular in:

- **nuclear reactor components** (элементы атомных реакторов),
- **pressure vessels** (баки под давлением),
- **pipes carrying explosive substances** (трубы со взрывоопасными веществами),
- and other areas.

The description states that in modern society technical diagnostics and non-destructive testing are called upon to ensure one of the primary vital conditions — the condition of safety of life activity. Methods and means of technical diagnostics are an inseparable part of the stages of development, manufacture and operation of technical objects. Errors at the development stage and malfunctions during operation, connected with undetected flaws of technical systems, devices and equipment, or with poor-quality materials used for manufacturing critical articles, always lead to serious consequences. In industrially developed countries, **losses from fatigue failure and corrosion of industrial objects alone amount to hundreds of billions of dollars per year**; according to expert estimates, this indicator has a still higher value in Russia.

## 2. Background art / prior art and its shortcomings

### 2.1 Ultrasonic flaw detection — 31.57 % of the market [1]

The most widespread method of local flaw detection today is the ultrasonic method, occupying **31.57 % of the market share** [1]. The description lists the following substantial shortcomings:

1. **Cannot give the real size of the flaw.** As a rule, ultrasonic flaw detection cannot answer the question of the real dimensions of a flaw — only of its **reflectivity in the direction of the receiver**. These quantities correlate, but not for all types of flaws.
2. **Some flaws are practically impossible to reveal** by the ultrasonic method by virtue of their character, shape or location in the object under inspection.
3. **Coarse-grained metals.** It is practically impossible to carry out reliable ultrasonic testing of metals with a coarse-grained structure, such as **cast iron** or an **austenitic weld seam (of thickness over 60 mm)**, because of the large scattering and strong attenuation of ultrasound.
4. **Small or complex-shaped parts** are difficult to inspect.
5. **Welded joints of dissimilar steels** (for example austenitic steels joined to pearlitic steels) are difficult to inspect ultrasonically, in view of the extreme inhomogeneity of the weld-seam metal and the base metal.
6. **Bulk and power dependence.** Equipment for ultrasonic flaw detection, although relatively mobile, nevertheless has large dimensions and a substantial dependence on sources of electrical energy.
7. **No semi-autonomous permanent-monitoring sensors.** In view of a certain complexity and the features of operation, it does not appear possible to create, on the basis of ultrasonic flaw detectors, semi-autonomous sensors or alarm systems for constant monitoring of operating systems.
8. **Radiation environments.** The use of ultrasonic systems is difficult under conditions of elevated radiation, for example in the active zone (core) of nuclear reactors or in other hazardous zones.
9. **Vibration.** The use of ultrasonic systems on objects during their operation is difficult, especially on pipe mains or load-bearing structures, because of the vibration present.

### 2.2 X-ray flaw detection — about 30 % of the market [1]

Another possible approach may be the use of X-ray flaw detection, which occupies of the order of **30 % of the market** [1]. However, despite its high accuracy, this method is extremely complex in application and its field of application is substantially limited.

### 2.3 Optical method — 21 % of the market [1]

A widespread method of flaw detection is also the optical method — **21 % of the market** [1] — but for obvious reasons this method has a very narrow field of application.

### 2.4 Electrical flaw detection — about 17.64 % of the market [2]

A different approach is the use of methods of **electrical flaw detection**, to which the method underlying the present invention also belongs. This method is less popular than the two described above and occupies of the order of **17.64 % of the market** [2].

Methods of electromagnetic flaw detection may conditionally be divided into **passive** and **active**:

- **Passive methods** are based on measuring such parameters as **capacitance** or **electrical resistance**. Such methods possess low accuracy; substantial difficulties arise in developing amplifiers for such systems, and also in working with inhomogeneous materials, in particular weld seams.
- **Active methods** are based on **applying a voltage or passing an electric current** through the object under investigation, with simultaneous measurement of various quantities. By a number of constructional features these are the **closest analogues** to the claimed solution. Examples cited: **US 8466687 B2, US 5486767 A, JP H1090211 A** and others. These methods possess high accuracy and a wide field of application, and in many branches of industry successfully replace ultrasonic, X-ray-structural and optical methods.

**Shortcoming of the active methods:** because of the necessity of passing an electric current or applying an electrical voltage, their application becomes impossible, for example, when investigating pressure vessels containing explosive substances, pipe weld seams in oil-and-gas mains in service, or the active zone of nuclear reactors — in general in any field where passing a current or applying a voltage is extremely dangerous and is not permitted.

### 2.5 Object of the invention

The object of the present invention is the development of a **fundamentally new method of passive electromagnetic flaw detection, based on measuring the contact potential difference, which does not require passing an electric current or applying a voltage**. Accordingly the object may also be reformulated as **an expansion of the arsenal of technical means and an expansion of the field of application of such means**.

A further object is the **creation of various devices for detecting local flaws** using this method.

### Terms and definitions given in the description

- **Electrophysical transducers, contacts, contact-transducers** are equivalent terms throughout the description.
- **Contact potential difference (CPD)** in the present description is the potential difference between two points of the object of diagnosis, which arises as a result of **loading of the object** and in view of **quantum-mechanical effects**.
- **Object under inspection** in the present description is a metallic object for which it is necessary to determine the presence or absence of local flaws, their type and size. Such an object may be a **pressure vessel**, a **weld seam**, a **leg (stanchion) of an offshore oil platform at great depth**, etc.

### 5.4 Physics of the bimetallic contact used as the sensitive element

The **electrical properties of the bimetallic contact** of the transducer with the surface depend on:

- the quality of machining and cleanliness of the surfaces,
- the **electron density in the near-surface layer**,
- the features of the **electronic structure of the metals**,
- and a number of other factors,

which allows the electrical contact to be used as a **sensitive element**. The sensitive element serves to obtain **primary information about the state of the object**; an important function of the sensitive element is the **conversion of an external action into electrical signals**.

The interaction of the electrophysical transducer with the surface is described by **statistical laws**. Depending on the roughness of the surfaces and the magnitude of the applied load, the size of the spots of real contact is **from 0.1 to 10–40 µm**. On these spots, pressures arise reaching **10–20 % of the theoretical strength of the material** [3]. With increasing load, the growth of the area of real contact occurs mainly through an **increase in the number of spots**, with insignificant growth of their sizes. When using the contact potentiometry method, another important question is the **spectral analysis of diagnostic signals** [4–7].

The **area of real bimetallic contact** is from **10⁻⁴ to 10⁻¹ of the nominal contact area** and even at large loads **does not exceed 40 %**. For a pair of metals with different hardness, the area of actual contact is determined by the **properties of the softer metal** and by the **geometry of the surface of the harder one** [8, 9].

In the case of contact of a metallic transducer with a surface bearing **oxide films, organic and other contaminants**, a so-called **ohmic contact** is formed [10]. In this case no potential barrier is formed at the **"metal–dielectric" interface** if the **work function** of the electron of the metal is **less than the electron affinity energy of the semiconductor**. The presence and height of the barrier also depend on the **density and energy distribution of the surface electron-hole states of the semiconductor**, formed because of the presence of foreign atoms on the surface. There is another point of view, which is that when an ohmic contact is formed, **injection of electrons from the metal into the dielectric** takes place.

When this method is used, the possibility appears of **investigating local surface phenomena and changes in the morphology of the surface layer**.

### 5.5 Preferred embodiment of the method — stage 1: surface preparation and contact application

At the initial stage it is necessary to carry out **preparation of the surface** — cleaning the surface under investigation of **paint, rust, deposit** — of any coating that may become an obstacle to contact with the object of diagnosis. However, a variant is also possible in which the **contacts (2) of the meter are needles**; then cleaning of the surface is not required — it is only necessary to apply a certain **pressure on the needles** so that they push through the layer of paint or rust and deposit.

After this, the **contacts (2) of the measuring device are applied to the surface — at least two, and in the preferred variant three**. In the case of **semi-automatic monitoring** the contacts (2) may be considerably more numerous, depending on the required area of inspection; a variant is possible in which the contacts (2) entirely **cover a certain surface or an important assembly** of the object of diagnosis.

In another variant of realization it is possible to **install the measuring contact-transducers on the object of diagnosis in advance** (semi-automatic monitoring), provided with a system for transmitting information, and to fix them there. After that, further intervention of an operator is not required — the contacts take off the data and transmit them to the processing centre. Transmission of such data may be effected either by **wired** or by **wireless** means. A variant is also possible in which the data are recorded in a **ROM (permanent storage device, ПЗУ)** and periodically taken off by an operator. The ROM may in this case be made in the format of a **"black box"**, that is, **impact-resistant and/or heat-resistant**.

### 5.6 Stage 2: measurement and scanning

At the second stage — the **measurement stage** — the signal of the **potential difference between the contacts (2)** is taken off **stage by stage**, together with **movement of the contacts (2) — scanning**. For effecting movement of the contacts (scanning), the **optimal shape of the contacts (2) is in the form of metallic rollers**.

In the other variant of realization (**semi-automatic monitoring**) scanning and movement may not be carried out at all; in this case only a **previously specified section of the object** is monitored, for example a **weld seam** or a **load-bearing assembly/part**.

After the value of the potential difference between the contacts (2) has been taken off in the form of an **analog signal**, this signal is subjected to **hardware amplification by means of the amplifier (4)**, arrives at the **ADC (5)** and is transmitted to the **processing module (9)** already in **digital form**. Transmission of the signal may be effected by either **wired or wireless** method, using the available means of reception/transmission **(8, A, Б)**.

A variant of realization is possible in which the **intermediate digital signal is processed by the digital information processing unit (6)** and displayed by means of the means (7). For example, this may be a **roughly processed signal** giving a preliminary idea of the presence or absence of flaws; however, variants are possible in which unit (6) carries out **more detailed processing of the data**.

The possibility is also provided of adding **systems for inputting information or control systems** to the flaw-detection devices in both described variants. Such systems may be realized either as **button panels** or as a **keyboard with a mouse**. In another variant, the means (7) may be a **touch screen**.

### 5.7 Signal processing and signal extraction

Owing to the **small energy of the mechanical waves formed**, the useful signal has a **small amplitude and is screened by the noise component**. To reduce the influence of the noise component, specially developed methods of signal processing are applied at the processing centre (Fig. 4). In that figure, the influence of the stages of successive loading of the specimen on the character of the change of the signal is visible: **each successive increase of the load leads to an increase of the signal in modulus, while removal of the load at the end of the experiment has the opposite effect**.

#### 5.7.1 Discrete Fourier transform (DFT)

The **discrete Fourier transform (ДПФ, DFT)** allows the transition from a discrete signal to a discrete spectrum and back, and possesses **good frequency localization**.

If the electrical signal at the output of the transducer is a function of time $\Delta\varphi(t)$ of finite duration ($0 \le t \le T_\text{к}$), and measurements during the time $T_\text{к}$ are made at equal time intervals

$$t = T_\text{к}/n,$$

then the discrete $N$-point Fourier transform for the function $\Delta\varphi(t)$ has the form:

$$\Phi(k)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Delta\varphi(n)\,e^{-i2\pi nk/N}. \qquad (1)$$

- $\Phi(k)$ — the frequency output of the DFT at the $k$-th point of the spectrum (dimensionless spectral amplitude, in the practical plots expressed in µV or mV);
- $k$ — spectral point index, $0 \le k \le N-1$ (dimensionless);
- $N$ — the number of samples used in computing the DFT (dimensionless);
- $\Delta\varphi(n)$ — the already discretized time function, i.e. the $n$-th sample (value) in the time domain, with $n$, like $k$, varying from zero to $N-1$; the potential difference, in µV;
- $T_\text{к}$ — the total duration of the measurement (h or s);
- $t$ — the sampling interval (h or s);
- $i$ — the imaginary unit.

The relationship between a discrete signal and its discrete transform has the form:

$$\Phi_n=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Delta\varphi_n\,e^{-i2\pi nk/N}, \qquad (2)$$

where the function $\Delta\varphi_n$ is presented in the form of a **vector**.

- $\Phi_n$ — the discrete transform of the signal, presented in vector form;
- $\Delta\varphi_n$ — the vector of samples of the potential difference, µV;
- $N$, $n$, $k$ — as in formula (1).

#### 5.7.2 Continuous wavelet transform (CWT)

The **continuous wavelet transform (НВП, CWT)** is an integral transform in which, under the integral sign, there stands the **scalar product of the diagnostic-signal function $\Delta\varphi(t)$ and the basis functions $\psi_{a,b}(x)$** of the form:

$$\psi_{a,b}(x)=a^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\,\psi\!\left(\frac{x-b}{a}\right), \qquad (3)$$

- $\psi_{a,b}(x)$ — the scaled and shifted basis function (wavelet), real-valued, defined on a certain interval;
- $\psi(x)$ — the unperturbed (mother) function;
- $b$ — the parameter intended for **shifting the function along the time axis** (same units as time);
- $a$ — the parameter for **shifting the function along the frequency axis** (scale parameter, dimensionless); **large values of $a$ correspond to low frequencies, small values to high frequencies**;
- $x$ — the argument (time variable).

The continuous wavelet transform is then represented in the form:

$$W(a,b)=a^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi\!\left(\frac{x-b}{a}\right)\Delta\varphi(x)\,dx. \qquad (4)$$

- $W(a,b)$ — the wavelet coefficient at scale $a$ and shift $b$;
- $\Delta\varphi(x)$ — the diagnostic signal (potential difference), µV;
- other symbols as in (3).

The **mathematical operation of multiplication by a window is contained in the basis function itself**, which allows the window to be narrowed or widened.

#### 5.7.3 Properties of the wavelet decomposition exploited for diagnosis

A detailed calculational procedure for signal processing can be found in works [10, 11]. The **higher the level of decomposition, the higher the frequency of the harmonics**. Wavelet coefficients serve, in the discrete transform, for **scaling and localization in time** of the wavelet functions participating in the decomposition.

By means of a **wavelet filter** it is possible to reveal **local features of signals with their reference to time or to spatial coordinates**. The more sharply the "feature" of the signal is expressed, the more strongly it stands out on the spectrogram and the higher the levels of the wavelet coefficients. Wavelet spectrograms distinctly single out such details as **small discontinuities of the signal, a change of sign of the first and second derivatives, a change of the frequency of harmonics in time**, etc.

In mechanical tests, **wavelet filters effectively single out from the spectrum the harmonics associated with the relaxation of elastic stresses**. In this case **local components appear in the detected signal**. In the spectrum, a **successive appearance of "doubled" pulses having opposite signs with high values of amplitude** is often observed.

The **wavelet spectrum gives a complete restoration of the initial signal**. During restoration, filtering may be applied with a varying level of detail, which allows, for example, exclusion of the high-frequency harmonics of the coefficients while singling out separate fragments.

#### 5.7.4 Fitness criterion and the three conditional states

To the results of fatigue tests, a **criterion of fitness of the article (the signature criterion, сигнатурный критерий)** and **limiting values of parameters in the diagnostic space** are applied.

For **thin duralumin plates with stress concentrators**, tested for cyclic fatigue on the **DTU (ДТУ) stand** [14, 15], the basis of such a criterion is the **value of the length of the visible growing crack**.

The interval of amplitudes of the diagnostic signal was divided into **three sub-ranges** corresponding to three conditional states:

1. **the object under inspection is serviceable** (absence of visible cracks);
2. **the object under inspection is partially unserviceable** (crack growth at the nucleation stage, crack length **tens-to-hundreds of µm**);
3. **the object under inspection is unserviceable** (propagation of a crack leading to destruction of the specimen, crack length **of several mm**).

The amplitude-frequency characteristic of the signal was obtained for each sub-range by means of the **fast Fourier transform** (figure 7). As is evident from the results of the spectral analysis, **the AFC of a serviceable object and the AFC of a deliberately unserviceable object with visually noticeable structural damage (cracks) in the region of the stress concentrator differ by more than an order of magnitude**.

## Stated technical result

As printed in the abstract: **"The technical result consists in a significant expansion of the field of application with a simultaneous increase in accuracy."**

As elaborated in the description (p. 3):

1. **Principal technical result** — because the task of creating such a method and the devices for realizing it is being solved for the first time, the main technical result consists in **realizing the purpose of the invention itself: the detection of local flaws** by a passive method based on the contact potential difference, with no electric current passed and no voltage applied to the object.
2. **Substantial expansion of the field of possible application** — use is made possible in the active zone of nuclear reactors, in pressure vessels, in pipe joints of oil-and-gas mains, under high pressure or in a strongly rarefied medium, and in premises with an elevated radiation background, i.e. under conditions of limited access.
3. **Increase in accuracy** relative to ultrasonic methods for some cases of application.
4. **Independence from sources of wired electrical power supply** for all fields of application.
5. **The possibility of creating semi-autonomous and autonomous flaw-detection systems.**
6. Reformulated object: **expansion of the arsenal of technical means** for flaw detection and **expansion of the field of application** of such means.
