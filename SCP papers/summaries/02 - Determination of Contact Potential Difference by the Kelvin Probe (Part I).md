## Abstract

Determination of electric potential difference using the Kelvin probe, i.e. the vibrating capacitor technique, is one of the most sensitive measuring procedures in surface physics. Periodic modulation of the distance between the electrodes leads to changes in capacitance, thereby causing current to flow through the external circuit. The procedure of contactless, non-destructive determination of contact potential difference between an electrically conductive vibrating reference electrode and an electrically conductive sample is based on precise control measurement of the Kelvin current flowing through a capacitor.

The present research is devoted to creation of a new low-cost miniaturised measurement system to determine potential difference in real time and at high measurement resolution. Furthermore, using the electrode of a reference probe, the Kelvin method leads both to the indirect measurement of an electronic work function, or a contact potential of the sample, and of a surface potential for insulator-type samples.

In the article, the first part of the research — the basic principles and prerequisites for establishment of such a measurement system — is considered.

Kelvin probe, contact potential difference, surface potential.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Volta's contact electricity and the Volta law of serial contacts

At the end of the 18th century A. Volta conducted experiments on metal pairs and discovered "contact electricity", which in modern language means the same as the **contact potential difference (CPD)** originating in the work-function difference of two dissimilar electro-conducting materials in contact.

Volta revealed [1] that if different metals are electrically connected in a sequential row and the temperature of all metals is equal, the potential difference between the two external metals of that row does not depend on the composition of the other metals in the line. This potential difference is also known as the **Volta potential**. If electrical contact is set between the outer end surfaces of the metals in the row, creating a closed loop, it follows from the Volta law of serial contacts that the electric driving force in this circuit equals zero and no current flows — unless the temperature of all metals composing the circuit is *not* equal (current does not flow as long as all temperatures are equal).

### 1.2 Energy-level explanation of the CPD

Electron energy-level diagrams (Fig. 1) are used to explain the occurrence of the contact potential difference.

Before being connected (Fig. 1a) the two metals are electrically neutral, and two metals having different work functions $\Phi_1$ and $\Phi_2$ share the same **local vacuum level** $E_v$. The work function $\Phi$ is defined as the minimum work needed to extract electrons from the Fermi level of a solid carrying a net charge [2], [3].

Upon short-circuiting (Fig. 1b) the electrons flow to the metal with the higher work function until uniform redistribution of electrons at the Fermi levels $E_{F1}$ and $E_{F2}$ of both metals is reached. This leads to the observation of the Volta (outer) potential difference $\Delta\Psi$, which is equivalent to the difference of the work functions of the two metals:

$$\Delta\Psi = (\Phi_1 - \Phi_2)/e \tag{1}$$

- $\Delta\Psi$ — Volta (outer) potential difference, i.e. the contact potential difference, V
- $\Phi_1, \Phi_2$ — work functions of metal 1 and metal 2, eV (energy units)
- $e$ — elementary charge, C

The resulting voltage $V_{CPD} = \Delta\Psi$ compensates the work-function difference by shifting the local vacuum level. (Fig. 1c, drawn for the biased/nulled state, labels the same quantity with the opposite sign convention, $\Delta\Psi = (\Phi_2 - \Phi_1)/e$, consistent with Eq. (3) below.)

Figure 1(c) demonstrates that the shift of the local vacuum level can be compensated by an external **"bucking voltage"** (sometimes spelled "backing voltage"):

$$V_B = -V_{CPD}$$

- $V_B$ — externally applied bucking (backing, compensating) voltage, V

Since the work function of metals is an intrinsic parameter, it remains constant after the connection of two different metals [4], while the electrochemical potential of electrons within both phases becomes identical.

If $V_B$ is chosen such that the electric field is eliminated (**the flat vacuum condition**), the Volta (outer) potential is

$$V_{CPD} = \Delta\Psi = (\Phi_1 - \Phi_2)/e$$

Here the potential $V_{CPD}$ in volts (V) is equal to the energy $(\Phi_1 - \Phi_2)$ in electron-volts (eV) divided by the elementary (proton/electron) charge $e$.

### 1.3 The Kelvin probe arrangement and the Fermi-level equilibrium model

Usually the measuring device with the Kelvin probe uses a metal (e.g. **gold**) as the probe, positioned within a short distance parallel to a flat sample material. If the sample is a conductor, the contact potential difference $\Delta\Psi$ is evaluated by Eq. (1). **The Kelvin method is based on the Fermi level equilibrium model.**

If the electrode of the Kelvin probe is a metal, the electron's exchange energy at the Fermi level inside the solid determines the bulk contribution of the main work function, defined as the minimum work needed to extract electrons from the Fermi level of a solid carrying no net charge [4]. It is accepted that the electron is removed to the end position (vacuum level) just outside the sample. In this case it can be assumed that the electrons inside the bulk of any conductor can move freely, the free electrons are located on the surface of the solid, the work function is mainly a **surface property**, and the CPD value is determined by Eq. (1).

### 1.4 Dependence of the measured CPD on surface conditions

The CPD between a sample and a probe (reference electrode) depends not only on the material work function but also on the **surface conditions**, such as contamination and deposition of a monolayer. Work is required for the electron to pass the barrier at the surface of a solid, which is made of:

- the **dipole layer** in the case of conductors;
- the **band bending** in the case of semiconductors.

This must be considered when studying materials at normal pressure **in moist air**, and when studying polycrystalline materials and insulators.

In semiconductors the Fermi level lies between the valence and conduction bands, and additional work is required to move the electron from the Fermi level to the conduction band.

If the sample is a metal or a semiconductor, charge may be formed not only at the sample surface but also **at the boundary between the sample and the conductive or semi-conductive substrate electrode**. Part of the charge can move through the sample volume, where sample polarisation and charge trapping (charge catching by the traps) are possible.

If insulators are involved, the measured value — apart from the chemical potential difference of the reference metal and the sample substrate metal — depends also on the insulator boundary surfaces and on the **thickness-dependent potential fall** on the insulator.

### 1.5 Decomposition of the work function

In all these cases a more common description of the work function is its presentation as the sum of the chemical work needed to transfer the electron from just outside the sample to the Fermi level ($\mu_e$, the chemical potential) and the electrostatic work to move an electron through the dipole layer ($\chi$, the dipole or surface potential), where $e$ is the elementary charge [4]:

$$\Phi = -\mu_e + e\cdot\chi \tag{2}$$

- $\Phi$ — electronic work function of the material, eV
- $\mu_e$ — chemical potential of the electron just inside the bulk, eV
- $\chi$ — dipole (surface) potential just outside the surface, V
- $e$ — elementary charge, C

The Kelvin capacitor's opposite materials (sample and gold reference) are connected to each other through the outer circuit, so that the electrochemical potentials of the electrons are in equilibrium. Therefore, the external potential difference at bucking voltage $V_B = 0$ (Fig. 1b) is [5]:

$$e(\Psi_1 - \Psi_2) = (\mu_{e1} - e\chi_1) - (\mu_{e2} - e\chi_2) = \Phi_2 - \Phi_1 \tag{3}$$

- $\Psi_1, \Psi_2$ — outer (Volta) potentials of materials 1 and 2, V
- $\mu_{e1}, \mu_{e2}$ — chemical potentials of the electrons in materials 1 and 2, eV
- $\chi_1, \chi_2$ — surface (dipole) potentials of materials 1 and 2, V

### 1.6 Contact potentials, surface potentials and work functions — distinguishing the measured quantities

In respect of the values measured by the Kelvin method it is important to distinguish whether the difference of **contact potentials**, the difference of **surface potentials**, or the difference of **work functions** is concerned [5]. These conceptualisations initially apply to **homogeneous surfaces**.

When one electrode of the Kelvin condenser is connected to the other, electron diffusion begins from the electrode material with the higher Fermi level to the material with the lower one. During level equalisation the electrical potential on the electrode with the lower Fermi level grows and decreases in the opposite case, so that the contact potential difference of the Kelvin condenser between the electrodes increases. Without this outer Volta potential, the potential leap — i.e. the electrical field — arises directly at the place where both condenser electrodes are connected, and it resists the process of electron diffusion. When this **inner** contact potential difference reaches a certain value, the electron diffusion stops and the Fermi levels of both electrode materials equalise. To let the electrical charge $e$ pass the phase boundary from the material "just inside the bulk" to one "outside the sample", it is necessary to do the work $e\chi$.

Thereby, the electronic work function $\Phi$ is the sum of the chemical potential just inside the bulk $\mu_e$ and the electrical potential outside the sample $\chi$, as in Eq. (2). The surface potential forms on the sample surface, but at a distance $l$ outside the surface it gradually decreases to zero, which is determined by the amount of free electrons compensating the charge carriers. The distance $l$ depends on the electrode material and on the presence of other surfaces and boundary phases, which could be located close enough to cause mutual influence — **ignoring this fact can lead to errors when interpreting measurement results**.

- For **metals**: the surface charge is compensated by the free electrons outside the material surface at a distance of some atomic diameters, forming a **few Å thick dipole layer**.
- For **semiconductors**: part of the compensating charge is located at an extended boundary layer of the space-charge zone; here the **Debye length $l = \delta_D$ can expand up to 1 μm** [5].

Therefore it is possible to split the work function into a purely chemical work $\mu_e$ and a purely electric work $e\chi$. On the other hand, it can also be divided into the difference of conduction and valence bands $E_C - E_F$, the band bending $eV_S$ and the electron affinity $\xi$ [5]:

$$\Phi = (E_C - E_F) - eV_S - \xi \tag{4}$$

- $E_C$ — conduction band edge energy, eV
- $E_F$ — Fermi level energy, eV
- $V_S$ — surface band bending potential, V
- $\xi$ — electron affinity, eV

### 1.7 The insulator case

Whereas for semiconductors the measured value of the contact potential difference of two materials can still be interpreted as a difference of two work functions, **this interpretation is not applicable to insulators**. For insulator samples without a chemical potential difference $\Delta\mu$ between the probe material and the insulator grounding metal, the measured potential difference $\Psi_{ISOL} - \Psi_{REF}$ also comprises the potentials on the insulator boundary surfaces $\chi_1, \chi_2$ and the thickness-dependent potential curve $V_S$ on the insulator [5]:

$$e(\Psi_{ISOL} - \Psi_{REF}) = (\mu_{eM} - e\chi_1 - e\chi_2 - V_S) - (\mu_{eREF} - e\chi_{REF}) = \Delta\mu - e(\chi_1 + \chi_2 + \chi_{REF}) - eV_S \tag{5}$$

- $\Psi_{ISOL}$ — outer potential of the insulator sample, V
- $\Psi_{REF}$ — outer potential of the reference electrode, V
- $\mu_{eM}$ — chemical potential of electrons in the sample substrate metal, eV
- $\mu_{eREF}$ — chemical potential of electrons in the reference electrode, eV
- $\chi_1, \chi_2$ — surface potentials on the two insulator boundary surfaces, V
- $\chi_{REF}$ — surface potential of the reference electrode, V
- $V_S$ — thickness-dependent potential fall (potential curve) on the insulator, V
- $\Delta\mu$ — chemical potential difference of probe material and insulator grounding metal, eV

### 1.8 Validity of Eq. (1) for the metal–semiconductor arrangement

It is worthwhile to examine the validity of relation (1) for a metal–semiconductor Kelvin probe arrangement in detail.

In an isolated semiconductor, the surface band bending is determined by the **free surface charge neutrality condition**

$$Q_{ss} + Q_{sc} = 0$$

- $Q_{ss}$ — net surface charge (per unit area)
- $Q_{sc}$ — net charge in the surface charge region (per unit area)

If a metal is connected to the back side of a semiconductor, a **MIS structure (metal–insulator–semiconductor)** is formed. It is then possible to analyse whether the semiconductor band bending — and thus its work function — is changed. The appropriate charge neutrality condition becomes

$$Q_m + Q_{ss} + Q_{sc} = 0$$

- $Q_m$ — charge density on the metal + insulator surface

The effect of $Q_m$ may be significant if the width of the capacitor gap is **not much larger than the width of the surface depletion region** [6]. Therefore the potential energy drop across the capacitor gap may be modified to

$$eV'_{CPD} = (\Phi'_1 - \Phi_2)$$

instead of $eV_{CPD} = (\Phi_1 - \Phi_2)$, where $\Phi'_1$ is the modified work function of the semiconductor.

**However, the magnitude of the external DC bias voltage $V_B$ required to discharge the capacitor remains equal and opposite to the real contact potential difference $V_{CPD}$ rather than to $V'_{CPD}$.** This happens because if the capacitor is discharged, $Q_m$ is by definition zero, the charge neutrality condition reduces to that of a free surface, and the semiconductor work function returns to its original value.

### 1.9 Historical development: Kelvin's null method and Zisman's vibrating capacitor

Nearly a hundred years passed after Volta had demonstrated contact "electricity" before **Lord Kelvin, in 1898** [7], discovered the possibility of measuring the CPD quantitatively. He used a condenser with plates of two different metals together with an electrometer; this was the first time the direct measurement of CPD was carried out by compensating the measured potential difference with a variable outer voltage source — the **null method**.

**Zisman first applied this principle in the form of the vibrating capacitor in 1932** [8], which, compared with a manual compensation arrangement, sped up CPD measurement several times; only then did the method become widely applied in practice. In this case the contact potential difference between the Kelvin probe and the conductive sample is equal to the applied compensation voltage.

### 1.10 Areas of application

Determination of the surface electric potential difference by the Kelvin probe (vibrating capacitor technique) is one of the most sensitive measuring procedures in surface physics. Kelvin probe force microscopes and scanning tunnelling microscopes have been modified to obtain high-resolution data of the electric surface potential distribution.

Due to its extremely high surface sensitivity, the method is used in many areas of modern chemistry and physics to measure and analyse the surface characteristics of: conductors, semiconductors, insulators, chemicals, biochemicals, photochemicals, chemical sensors, biosensors, biochemical microarrays, electronic image devices, micromachined devices, nanodevices, corroded materials, coatings, adsorbed materials, contaminated materials, oxides, thin films, liquid interfaces, self-assembling monolayers and others [9]. It is also applied to investigate **semiconductor doping** [10] and to study **energy-level alignment of conjugated polymers** deposited on various electrodes [11]. Further applications were provided to measure and analyse different organic molecules and thin films [12]. The method is also used for high-resistivity non-polar and polar molecules deposited on both **gold and ITO glass electrodes**; the voltage drop over the capacitor formed by an organic layer and reference electrode is given in [13].

## 2. Basic Principles Underlying the Measuring System

### 2.1 The Kelvin capacitor and its capacitance

The Kelvin method is one of the most commonly used for CPD measuring. This contactless, non-intrusive method is based on the traditional Kelvin probe (Fig. 2a) placed plane-parallel to the stationary sample electrode at a distance $d_0$, shaping a simple two-plate capacitor.

To measure the CPD, in accordance with the law of Volta's serial contacts, it is necessary to connect a variable voltage source $V_B$ and a series resistor $R_{IN}$ via an external circuit (Fig. 2a). Due to the difference in work functions of the different electrode materials, **the CPD must be measured in an open circuit**, i.e. using a dielectric such as vacuum or air between the surfaces of the inner plates, $V_{CPD} = \Delta\Psi$. To improve the resolution of a sample surface scan, **a small probe surface is required**.

The orientation of the Kelvin probe surface area with respect to the sample surface is analogous to a parallel-plate capacitor, whose capacitance $C$ is defined:

$$C = (\varepsilon_o \cdot \varepsilon_r \cdot A)/d_0 \tag{6}$$

- $C$ — capacitance of the Kelvin capacitor, F
- $\varepsilon_o$ — dielectric constant (vacuum permittivity), F/m
- $\varepsilon_r$ — relative dielectric constant of the gap medium; **for measurements in air or vacuum it is assumed to be 1**
- $A$ — area of the Kelvin probe, m²
- $d_0$ — distance between the plates, m

### 2.2 Charge on the Kelvin capacitor

If the voltage $V_{CPD}$ caused by the Kelvin condenser inner surface potentials is constant, the condenser is charged to charge $Q$:

$$Q = C \cdot V_{CPD} \tag{7}$$

- $Q$ — charge stored on the Kelvin capacitor, C

### 2.3 Vibration of the probe and the modulation factor

The Kelvin probe is stimulated into a **sinusoidal vibration** around the average distance $d_0$ between the Kelvin probe electrode and the sample surface, with amplitude $d_1$ (with the constraint $d_1 < d_0$) and angular frequency $\omega$ expressed in radians. The probe location changes with time:

$$d(t) = d_0 + d_1 \cdot \sin(\omega t) \tag{8}$$

- $d(t)$ — instantaneous probe–sample spacing, m
- $d_0$ — mean (average) spacing, m
- $d_1$ — vibration amplitude, m, with $d_1 < d_0$
- $\omega$ — angular vibration frequency, rad/s
- $t$ — time, s

The ratio of $d_1$ to $d_0$ characterises the amplitude of the current signal and is referred to as the **modulation factor** $m$:

$$m = d_1 / d_0 \tag{9}$$

- $m$ — dimensionless modulation factor (index of modulation)

### 2.4 The Kelvin (vibrating-capacitor) current

It follows from Eqs. (6) and (7) that the charge $Q$ changes along with changes of the Kelvin probe capacity, resulting in a change of current $i(t)$ in the outer circuit, which at bucking voltage $V_B = 0$ and $V_{CPD} \neq 0$ reproduces the current:

$$i(t) = dQ/dt = V_{CPD}\cdot dC/dt \tag{10}$$

- $i(t)$ — Kelvin current in the external circuit, A (the plotted values in Fig. 2b are of the order of picoamperes)

Substituting Eqs. (6)–(8) into Eq. (7), the current signal $i(t)$ is expressed as (reproduced exactly as printed in the paper):

$$i(t) = V_{CPD}\cdot \varepsilon_0 \cdot \varepsilon_r \cdot A \cdot [m\cdot\omega\cdot\cos(\omega t)] / [1 + m\cdot\sin(\omega t)^2] \tag{11}$$

- All symbols as defined above; the numerator carries the $m\omega\cos(\omega t)$ term produced by differentiating the time-varying gap, and the denominator carries the squared gap-modulation term that makes the waveform increasingly non-sinusoidal as $m$ grows.

### 2.5 The conventional nulling technique

In the conventional nulling technique [14], the variable bucking potential $V_B$ is adjusted until the Kelvin current vanishes, $i \to 0$. Then $V_B$ is equal and opposite to $V_{CPD}$, the electric field between the Kelvin capacitor electrodes is compensated, and the zero output signal is recorded, wherein the ac current in the Kelvin probe outer circuit drops to zero:

$$i(t) = (V_{CPD} + V_B)\cdot dC/dt = 0 \quad \text{if} \quad V_{CPD} = -V_B$$

Determination of the voltage $V_{CPD}$ caused by a contact potential difference on the vibrating electrode of the capacitor in systems with a Kelvin probe is associated with introducing the compensating voltage $V_B$ into the capacitor external circuit, at which the Kelvin current $i(t)$ disappears. In this case the absolute value of the signal $V_{CPD}$ comes into balance with the value of the voltage $V_B$, and the polarity of $V_{CPD}$ is opposite to the polarity of $V_B$.

### 2.6 Why null detection is difficult — the off-null algorithm

**The exact measurement of the equilibrium point $V_{CPD}$ is made more complex by the fact that in this region the signal-to-noise ratio reaches its minimum as $i(t) \to 0$, and the noise creates a biasing voltage, thereby causing measurement errors.**

Therefore, present conventional $V_{CPD}$ measuring systems use the **"off-null" algorithm**, where the Kelvin probe signal is measured at **two or more values of $V_B$**, for instance at high signal levels, and the balance point is obtained by **extrapolation**.

### 2.7 Disadvantages of the accurate nulling / off-null technique

The authors list the drawbacks of this accurate nulling technique:

- The statistical **linear correlation–regression analysis**, despite its high complexity in the case of an area scan with **a few hundred measurement points**, first of all requires completing a full set of all measurements.
- Consequently, **online (real-time) monitoring of the contact potential difference is not possible**, and the result of the measurement can be analysed only after the experiment is finished.
- Implementation of the modified off-null technique is generally associated with the need for a **PC with pre-installed extensive software** and use of an **external data acquisition digital control unit with a host-PC data acquisition system** housed in the PC system.
- This not only increases the extent and the weight of the measuring equipment, but also raises production costs and therefore **limits the wider use of the Kelvin probe**.

### 2.8 The proposed new approach

The authors indicate "a new and simple way that allows determining the contact potential difference by entering the **composite and time-varying bucking voltage** in the Kelvin probe measuring circuit."

The results of the experimental measurement system test for determination of the contact surface potential difference **in the range of +10,000 V up to –10.000 V** (as printed) have shown a **stable measurement resolution in the range of ± (1–2 mV)**. The details are deferred to Part 2 of the article, to be published in one of the upcoming journals.

**Measurement conditions / error sources discussed by the authors**

- The CPD must be measured in an **open circuit**, with vacuum or air as the dielectric between the inner plates.
- **Small probe area** is required to improve the resolution of a surface scan.
- The measured CPD depends on **surface conditions** — contamination and monolayer deposition — not only on the bulk work function.
- Measurements at **normal pressure in moist air**, and on **polycrystalline materials and insulators**, require the surface barrier (dipole layer for conductors, band bending for semiconductors) to be taken into account.
- **Proximity of other surfaces and boundary phases** within the decay distance $l$ of the surface potential causes mutual influence; ignoring it leads to interpretation errors.
- In metal/semiconductor samples, **charge at the sample–substrate boundary**, charge motion through the sample volume, **sample polarisation and charge trapping** affect the result.
- For insulators, **boundary-surface potentials and the thickness-dependent potential fall** enter the measured value.
- In the **MIS arrangement**, $Q_m$ is significant when the capacitor gap width is not much larger than the surface depletion region width; this modifies the potential energy drop across the gap to $eV'_{CPD}$, although the discharging bias $V_B$ still equals $-V_{CPD}$.
- At the null point, the **signal-to-noise ratio is minimum** because $i(t) \to 0$, and noise creates a biasing voltage and hence measurement error.
- Conventional off-null implementations require a **PC with extensive pre-installed software plus an external data-acquisition digital control unit**, increasing bulk, weight and cost.

## Conclusions (as stated by the authors)

The basic characteristics and principles for determination of contact potential difference using the Kelvin probe and the preconditions for development of such a measurement system have been considered, namely:

- Under the conditions described, **the measured value is the difference between the outer potentials of the two materials of the Kelvin capacitor electrodes.** If these materials are metals or semiconductors, the measured value can be interpreted as a difference between the work functions of these materials. The charge can be formed not only at the sample surface, but also at the boundary between the sample and the conductive or semi-conductive substrate electrode. Part of the charge can move through the sample volume, where sample polarization and charge catching by the traps is possible. If insulators or semiconductors with low electrical conductivity are involved, the measured value — apart from the chemical potential difference of the reference metal and the sample substrate metal — also depends on the insulator boundary surfaces and the thickness-dependent potential fall on the insulator.
- **The main principles and conditions underlying the measuring systems and the assessment of their effectiveness** have been established.

The Latvian summary (*Kopsavilkums*) restates these points and adds that the authors have indicated a new and simple way of continuously determining the contact potential difference $V_{CPD}$ by regularly introducing a **composite, time-varying bucking voltage $V_B$** into the Kelvin probe measuring circuit (described in Part 2 of the article), and that experimental tests of the measuring system over the contact surface potential difference range from +10,000 V to −10.000 V showed a stable measurement resolution of ± (1–2 mV).
